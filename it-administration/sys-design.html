The core design loop

Frame the problem → clarify requirements → quantify demand → identify dependencies → compare designs → model failure → plan operations → validate assumptions → iterate. Whether you work in systems, cloud, networks or platform engineering, this decision-making pattern stays similar; requirements and technical trade-offs vary.

Throughout this guide, start by explaining why an architecture is appropriate. Draw systems, estimate workload, articulate alternatives and challenge failure assumptions before writing commands.

Chapter 01 — Systems as decision models

Begin by identifying the service boundary: what outcomes the organization needs, the users who depend on it, the components your team owns, and the external systems you cannot control. A box marked “application” is not a complete architecture. Trace a user request through DNS, authentication, network path, service, storage, logging, and recovery. Ask which components are on the critical path and which are asynchronous. A backup system is not normally on the request path, yet it is essential to the service's recoverability.

Draw at least three views. The context view names users, systems, and external providers; the logical view shows services and data flows; the deployment view shows hosts, zones, networks, and failure domains. Each view answers a different question. Do not bury architectural choices inside vendor icons. A design is understandable only when another engineer can explain where data lives, who can access it, and which events can interrupt it.

Use a disciplined decision loop: frame the objective; make uncertainty explicit; generate at least two viable options; test them against quality goals; choose a reversible first design when possible; record a decision and the evidence that could invalidate it. Architecture is not prediction of the perfect future. It is management of change under imperfect information. Keep a risk register with probability, impact, owner, mitigation and trigger for revisiting the decision.

Worked decision: A 40-person company wants “enterprise-grade” file sharing. Before choosing shared storage, determine concurrent users, file sizes, remote access, identity system, retention, recovery expectations, and staff available to operate it. Two NAS nodes may be more complex than a managed file service. Neither is universally correct: the operator's skill and recovery test results belong in the decision.

Exercise: Draw the full dependency chain for an employee signing in and retrieving a document. Mark every external dependency, trust boundary, failure domain and operational owner.

Chapter 02 — Requirements engineering and measurable contracts

Separate functionality from quality. “Users can upload a document” is a functional requirement. “99.9% of successful uploads complete within 3 seconds for files under 20 MB during the busiest hour” is a measurable quality requirement. Requirements written as “fast, scalable and highly available” cannot be tested, and they force engineers to guess. Identify stakeholders with conflicting preferences: finance wants predictability, security wants control, users want low latency, and operations wants simplicity.

Establish the design horizon and workload assumptions. Ask for active users, peak concurrency, request mix, data creation rate, data retention, expected growth, and unusual events. Peak-to-average ratio matters: 10,000 daily requests arriving smoothly is entirely different from 10,000 requests arriving in ten minutes. Define what the architecture is allowed to lose under failure: data, sessions, transient jobs, or some combination. Also decide which dependencies can be degraded or disabled to preserve the core experience.

Availability is not the same as durability. An availability target describes the time a service can be used; data durability describes the likelihood that acknowledged information survives. State SLI (the measurement), SLO (the team's target), and SLA (any external contractual promise) separately. RPO is maximum tolerable lost work measured in time; RTO is maximum acceptable restoration time. A nightly backup may violate a 15-minute RPO even when recovery completes within a one-hour RTO.

Worked decision: A payroll service has 200 users, a monthly peak, and strict financial correctness. Choose consistent writes, auditable changes, and strong recovery controls over aggressive horizontal scaling. A public news feed with millions of reads has very different priorities. Both are “enterprise systems,” but their designs should differ.

Exercise: Write ten testable acceptance criteria for a branch-office identity and file system, including recovery, capacity, access control, and operations. For each one, describe how a reviewer will verify it.

Chapter 03 — Architecture artifacts and communication

A design review should produce artifacts, not only a diagram. Start with a one-page problem statement, the critical user journeys, and quantified constraints. Add a context diagram and deployment diagram. Follow with data flow and trust-boundary maps, capacity estimates, failure analysis, an ADR (architecture decision record), and an explicit acceptance plan. Your diagram should distinguish logical roles from actual running instances: two service boxes in a diagram do not prove they occupy independent physical fault domains.

An ADR records context, options, decision, consequences, and when to reconsider. Write down rejected alternatives fairly. For example: a managed database costs more per month but reduces staffing and patching burden; self-hosting offers configuration control but imposes backups, failover testing, and on-call responsibility. The rationale should survive the departure of the author.

Communicate with levels of abstraction. Business stakeholders need service outcomes, cost, risk, and recovery commitments. Engineers need protocols, dependencies, sizing assumptions, and failure behavior. Operations needs runbooks, metrics, ownership, access and escalation. Every arrow on a diagram should be meaningful: request, event, replication, administration, monitoring or backup. Label sensitive data paths and where encryption begins and ends.

Worked decision: A proposed network diagram shows “two data centers” with synchronized databases. Reviewers must ask which site owns writes, how conflicts are prevented, how split brain is detected, what happens under a partition, and what recovery test demonstrates the intended behavior. Adding a second site is not equivalent to creating a resilient service.

Exercise: Create an ADR comparing a single virtual-machine deployment with a two-node application tier. Document estimated availability benefit, extra operating effort, session/state changes, and rollback plan.

Chapter 04 — Quantitative scalability and capacity planning

Begin with load, not technology. Distinguish requests per second (RPS), concurrency, payload size, CPU time per request, disk operations per request, database connection count, and network traffic. Compute daily volume and peak volume separately. If 120,000 requests occur per day, the average is 1.39 RPS, but a 20× burst reaches roughly 27.8 RPS. Sizing for the average creates predictable peak-hour outages.

Little's Law provides a first approximation: L = λW, where L is average in-flight work, λ is throughput and W is average time in the system, under steady-state assumptions. At 100 requests/s and 0.2-second average response time, approximately 20 requests are in flight. This is not a universal maximum-capacity formula; saturation, queues, burstiness and long-tail latency can invalidate naive extrapolation. Record latency percentiles such as p50, p95 and p99 rather than quoting only the mean.

Scale vertically when bigger hardware is simple and enough, horizontally when the application can distribute work. Horizontal scaling generally demands careful handling of state, shared persistence, idempotency, coordination, and network overhead. A load balancer can spread requests but cannot create database capacity. Caches may lower read load, yet introduce staleness and invalidation complexity. Queues absorb bursts only until arrival rate consistently exceeds processing capacity; then backlog grows without bound.

Worked sizing: Suppose 300 peak requests/s need 40 ms of CPU each. Total CPU demand is 12 CPU-seconds per second. At a target 60% busy, a rough baseline is 20 CPU cores before overhead, uneven distribution, redundancy and load-test margins. If each request additionally causes 3 database operations, the storage tier sees about 900 operations/s. Confirm both estimates with measurements rather than using them as procurement-ready sizing.

Exercise: Model an internal application with 500 active users, 15 requests/minute each at peak, 50 KB response payloads and a 3× burst factor. Calculate peak RPS, approximate outbound network rate, concurrent requests at 250 ms, and the effect of losing one of three application nodes.

Chapter 05 — Reliability and recovery architecture

Availability depends on the end-to-end request path, not the number of redundant boxes shown. If two independent required components each achieve 99.9% availability, a simplistic series model gives 0.999 × 0.999 = 99.8001%, lower than either component alone. Real dependencies often share infrastructure and fail together, so an independence assumption must be questioned. Count common power, DNS, identity, storage, region, provider and human-change failure modes.

Active/passive redundancy trades failover time for simpler consistency; active/active can lower disruption but makes distributed writes, conflict resolution and health monitoring harder. Define failure detection thresholds and expected false positives. Explain how clients reconnect, how DNS TTL interacts with recovery, whether health checks measure genuine readiness, and how the system behaves when only part of a dependency graph fails. A second database node does not help if the primary's corruption immediately replicates to it.

Backups, replicas and snapshots serve distinct purposes. Replication supports continuity but can replicate deletion and corruption. Backups preserve recoverable historical copies; snapshots depend on storage semantics and often are not independent backups. Test a restore into an isolated environment, measure real restoration time, verify application consistency, and document the owner. Define error budgets: a 99.9% SLO over a 30-day period permits about 43.2 minutes of unavailability, assuming continuous service and a time-based measurement.

Worked decision: A branch authentication service needs to work during a WAN outage. Adding another cloud-hosted identity endpoint may not solve the branch's dependence on the WAN. Consider local authentication capability, cached credentials, offline application behavior, DNS dependencies and how identity changes reconcile after connectivity returns.

Exercise: Perform a failure mode and effects analysis: remove the primary DNS server, saturate the database disk, expire a TLS certificate, and disconnect a branch link. For each event record detection, customer impact, temporary mitigation, recovery, prevention and evidence.

Chapter 06 — Data systems and distributed trade-offs

Choose data storage by access patterns and correctness needs, not fashion. Relational databases excel when transactions and constraints matter; key-value systems serve high-throughput lookups; object stores handle durable blobs; caches trade freshness and complexity for speed. First identify the system of record, data ownership, read/write ratio, retention, indexing strategy, expected growth and restore procedure. Measure the data model's hottest query before deciding to add a cache or replica.

Replication and sharding solve different problems. Replication maintains multiple copies for availability or read scaling and raises lag and failover questions. Sharding distributes data across partitions for capacity but introduces routing, rebalancing and cross-partition query cost. Consistency is not simply “strong is good, eventual is bad.” A product catalog may tolerate temporarily stale reads; inventory allocation and payment ledgers often require stricter rules. CAP describes specific trade-offs during network partitions, not a free choice of any two desirable properties in normal operation.

Design changes for compatibility. Database migrations should support rolling deployments where old and new app versions briefly coexist. Use expand-and-contract: add a nullable field or new representation, deploy compatible readers/writers, backfill, then remove the old field after verification. Asynchronous jobs need retry policies, idempotency keys, poison-message handling and observable dead-letter queues. At-least-once delivery means duplicates are expected unless consumers make effects idempotent.

Worked decision: A customer search service adds a cache with a five-minute TTL. A user changes a record and immediately searches again. Decide whether read-your-writes consistency is required, and if so whether to invalidate or bypass the cache. The “fast” design becomes unacceptable if correctness expectations are unspecified.

Exercise: Compare PostgreSQL with object storage plus a metadata index for employee documents. Explain consistency, access patterns, permissions, backup, retention, search, cost and migration behavior.

Chapter 07 — Network and dependency design

Think in traffic paths and failure domains. Every service request traverses name resolution, address selection, routing, security policy, transport and application protocol. Map north–south traffic (entering/leaving the environment) and east–west traffic (between internal services). VLANs and subnets can limit broadcast scope and support segmentation, but they are not security controls by themselves; enforce policy with routers, firewalls, identity and least privilege.

Do not treat load balancing as a generic reliability stamp. An L4 device distributes connections; an L7 reverse proxy can route by request attributes and manage HTTP behavior. Readiness checks must verify required dependencies without amplifying outages. Timeouts, retries and circuit breakers can worsen overload if each caller multiplies requests during a failure. Set retry budgets and jitter. Model single points of failure including certificates, DNS zones, VPN gateways, firewalls and upstream routes.

In hybrid or multi-site designs, address overlapping CIDR blocks, asymmetric routing, MTU limits, NAT, name resolution, IAM federation, and observability boundaries before declaring connectivity complete. An IPsec tunnel being “up” says little about actual application connectivity. Validate the end-to-end path, allowed flows, routing, failover and packet loss. A connection plan should also state which traffic must never cross the boundary.

Worked decision: Two branch offices must reach a shared app. Option A routes through headquarters; Option B reaches a cloud endpoint directly. Compare latency, security inspection, WAN dependencies, operational complexity, costs and whether local services must continue if HQ is unreachable.

Exercise: Draw a three-tier app with web, API and database subnets. Label permitted traffic, security inspection points, DNS resolution, outbound access and the effects of each gateway failure.

Chapter 08 — Threat-informed system design

Security architecture starts with assets and trust, not a long checklist. Identify sensitive information, privileged operations, data stores, credential paths and externally reachable components. Use STRIDE to examine spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege. For each threat, connect the mitigation to a specific boundary and then choose a way to test it.

Design identity separately for people, applications and automation. Prefer scoped service identities to shared credentials; use short-lived credentials where feasible; control authorization at the resource boundary rather than trusting the network alone. Secrets require storage, rotation, revocation and an emergency response plan. Encrypt data in transit and at rest while thinking carefully about where keys live and who can request decryption. Key loss is a recoverability risk; key compromise is a confidentiality risk.

Security controls also affect resilience. A central authorization system can become a service dependency; a certificate authority outage can block new services; rate limits can protect backends but accidentally deny legitimate bursts. Plan break-glass access with logging and controls, design graceful failure modes, and test security policy changes like other infrastructure changes. “Zero trust” is a strategy of continuous verification and least privilege, not a product you install.

Worked decision: A service needs to read customer data and send audit events. Give it read-only database access and write-only audit access. Consider what happens if audit storage goes down: must transactions stop, queue locally, or continue with a degraded compliance posture? The decision depends on regulatory and business requirements.

Exercise: Threat-model an externally reachable document portal. Identify at least eight threats, affected assets, practical mitigations, residual risks and observable signals.

Chapter 09 — Operations, evidence and economics

A design is incomplete without an operator's perspective. Decide who owns the service, how deployment happens, what signals indicate user impact, and what can be restored without the original engineer. Metrics answer trends and utilization, logs explain discrete events, and traces connect work across services. Collect golden signals—latency, traffic, errors and saturation—and map them to customer journeys. Prefer alerts on actionable symptoms and avoid paging for every transient resource spike.

Capacity planning is a continuous measurement loop: record baseline demand, growth, seasonality, lead time to add capacity and safety margin. An autoscaler cannot react faster than instances can start and become healthy. Cloud bills expose the cost of idle redundancy, inter-region traffic, backups, logging, support and data egress. Total cost of ownership also includes administrator time, licensing, training and expected incident cost—not just the invoice for servers.

Test operational readiness with game days. Introduce safe, planned failures in a lab, confirm alerts fire, identify owners, follow runbooks and observe whether recovery time meets expectations. A service that recovers only because its creator remembers an undocumented step is not reliably operable. Track unplanned work and toil: repeated manual remediation is a signal to simplify or automate.

Worked decision: A monitoring team wants to retain every debug event forever. Estimate ingestion rate × average event size × retention period, plus indexes and replicas. Apply tiered retention and sensitivity rules instead of accepting unlimited volume. Better observability does not require unlimited spending or unrestricted storage of private data.

Exercise: Propose an operations plan for a 24/7 business application with three severity levels, on-call ownership, error-budget reporting, capacity forecast, backup tests and monthly operational review.

Chapter 10 — Design reviews and architecture interviews

In an interview, resist the urge to immediately name services. Clarify the users, use cases, load shape, geographic scope, data sensitivity, availability expectation and cost boundary. State assumptions aloud and check whether the interviewer agrees. Draw a minimum viable architecture first; then walk through how data moves and what fails. Extend it only when a requirement demands added complexity. Good design answers are coherent and testable rather than maximalist.

Quantify the high-level design with a back-of-envelope model. Estimate peak request rate, storage growth, bandwidth, database operations and failure-domain coverage. Then highlight where estimates are uncertain. Compare two plausible architectures against explicit criteria; make a recommendation; explain why the rejected option could still be appropriate under different conditions. It is fine to say “I would prototype or benchmark this assumption.” It is not fine to present an unsupported number as proven capacity.

Run a hostile review on your own design: What breaks if DNS fails? What happens when one zone disappears? What if a downstream call stalls? What happens during a schema migration when half the application fleet is on the old version? Can backups actually restore access? What is the most dangerous human mistake? Finally discuss growth triggers: add read replicas when measured database reads exceed a threshold, or revisit shard architecture when operationally validated single-node capacity is approached.

Worked interview: “Design a shared document platform for 2,000 employees.” A strong opening asks about upload size, collaboration needs, permissions, concurrent usage, search, retention, recovery and remote access. Only then does it consider object storage, metadata DB, upload API, background indexing, identity, cache, backups and monitoring. Explain the simplest deployment that meets the stated requirements.

Exercise: Give yourself 35 minutes: 5 to clarify, 8 to quantify, 10 to draw, 7 to examine failures and trade-offs, 5 to summarize assumptions and next steps. Record and critique your answer.

Seven questions to ask in a design interview

Who uses it? What must it do? How many users/requests? How fast and reliable? What must remain secure? How much can we spend? What can fail, and how would we know and recover?
